Is ChatGPT Safe for Your Child? What Nairobi Parents Need to Know

ChatGPT was not designed for children and has no parental dashboard. A practical guide for Nairobi families on whether, when and how to let a child use it.

If your child has asked to use ChatGPT, or you found it already open on their phone, you are not unusual. Kenya has the highest rate of ChatGPT use of any country in the world: 42.1% of internet users aged 16 and over used it in the past month, according to the DataReportal and Meltwater Global Digital Report 2025. Whatever your household decides, your child is growing up surrounded by people who use it.

The short answer is that ChatGPT is not inherently dangerous, but it was not built for children. There is no meaningful age verification, no parental dashboard, no content filter tuned for a ten-year-old, and no way to review what your child typed without looking over their shoulder. Those are design facts, not scare stories, and they are what your decision has to work around.

Common Sense Media, which studies children and media in the United States, found that 60% of parents are concerned about AI generating inappropriate content. Their more recent census of AI use among tweens and teens found that around one in six children who have used AI chatbots has encountered inappropriate material, and only about a third told a trusted adult when it happened.

That last number is the one worth sitting with. The risk is not only what a child might see. It is that they are unlikely to mention it, which means a household that has never opened the subject will not hear about it either.

The same research found that while roughly three quarters of children say their school told them when they may use AI, only about half say they were taught to use it safely, and only about half were taught how to judge whether an answer is accurate. Access is being handled. Judgement largely is not.

Confident wrong answers. This is the most common problem and the least dramatic. These tools produce fluent text whether or not the content is correct, and fluency reads as authority to an adult, let alone a twelve-year-old. A child researching a school project has no way to tell a well-sourced fact from a confident invention.

Content nobody intended. A curious child will test the boundaries of anything, and the tools are inconsistently guarded. This is usually curiosity rather than intent, which is exactly why punishment is the wrong first response.

Quiet dependence. The slowest risk and the one parents notice last. A child who reaches for the tool before thinking never builds the habit of forming their own position first. Nothing looks wrong for months, and then they cannot start a piece of work without it.

Use it yourself for a week, on something real. Not a test question. Ask it to help with something you actually need. You will learn more about where it is useful and where it is confidently wrong than any article can tell you, and you will be able to have a specific conversation rather than a nervous one.

Then ask your child to show you how they use it. Not as an interrogation. Genuinely watch. Most parents are surprised, in both directions.

Use it in the open. Shared spaces, not behind a bedroom door. This is the same rule most families already have for the internet and it works for the same reason.

No personal information goes in. Not their full name, school, home area, photographs or anything about other people. Practise the substitution with them once and it becomes automatic.

Think first, then ask. Before opening the tool, they write down what they already think in their own words, even one line. This one habit does more than any filter, because it keeps them the author of the work.

Anything surprising gets mentioned, not hidden. Say plainly that they will not be in trouble for what a tool said to them. Given only about a third of children currently tell an adult, this needs saying out loud rather than assuming.

Under 8: not directly. Whatever value there is can be had with an adult driving.

8 to 10: alongside an adult, on creative tasks, with the child's own idea coming first. This is where a habit is either formed or missed.

11 to 13: supervised independent use, with an explicit focus on checking answers. This is the right age to learn that the tool can be wrong, because they are old enough to test it and enjoy catching it out.

14 and up: more independence, and a real conversation about academic honesty, since by this point the school work has consequences attached.

Whatever rules you set, one question does most of the work: can you explain this to me in your own words, without the screen?

A child who can has learned something, regardless of what helped them produce it. A child who cannot has handed in something that is not theirs, regardless of how good it looks. That test is simple enough to use every week, and it does not require you to understand the technology at all.
